# OBJECTIVES

## 1. Priorities (in order)
1. **Achieve real commercial success.** Build a game with genuine market potential. Aim for meaningful sales and revenue, not as an optional bonus. Design around replayability, differentiation, player experience, creator/streamer potential, and a clear reason to buy.
2. **Ship a finished game on Steam.** Not just a prototype. The game must reach a polished, stable, coherent state and be released publicly on Steam. This is a core objective, not a hypothetical future step.
3. **Have fun building it together.** The project must stay enjoyable and motivating for both developers. Rapid experimentation and emergent situations are encouraged. The commercial goal must not kill the fun of making it.

## 2. Definition of success
Considered a success, even without becoming a major hit, if:
- The game is genuinely playable from start to finish.
- The core loop is fun and coherent.
- Multiplayer works reliably.
- The game reaches a production-ready state.
- It is released on Steam.
- It proves a very small team can take a game from idea to a real commercial release.
- We learned enough to understand what worked, what failed, and why.

Reaching Steam is the minimum success condition, not the ultimate ambition.

## 3. Ambition
A serious commercial indie project, not a casual side project. Aim: a game that looks simple on the surface but has enough depth, polish, systemic interaction, replayability and social/viral potential to compete with small-team Steam hits (R.E.P.O., PEAK, Lethal Company and similar). Quality high enough to be presented, marketed and sold as a serious product.

Optimize for:
- Fun before complexity.
- Strong core gameplay before content volume.
- Systemic interactions before scripted content.
- Replayability before long campaigns.
- Player-generated stories before heavy narrative.
- Polish where it affects perceived quality.
- Fast iteration and validation.
- Low production cost.
- Low technical complexity where complexity does not create player value.

Do NOT compete through content volume, AAA production value, large teams, complex backend, or unnecessary technical sophistication.

## 4. Time investment
Initial sprint: roughly two weeks of near full-time work.
- ~5 h/day, ~14 days.
- ~70 h per developer, ~140 combined human hours.

This assumes heavy use of AI and automation. Claude Code should accelerate C# implementation, Unity integration, boilerplate, debugging, refactoring, technical docs, tests, tooling and iteration. AI-assisted asset generation should cut manual work on 3D assets, environments, props, materials and textures where appropriate, animation where possible, and technical setup.

The 140 h is a human-time budget, not a measure of total output. AI-generated work must still be validated in-game. Speed never justifies accumulating untested or poorly understood systems.

## 5. Milestones
- **Playable prototype, ~1 week.** A playable multiplayer greybox with the essential loop, answering one question: is this actually fun? Prioritize core movement, core interactions, core rules, multiplayer, the main source of tension/fun, basic win/lose, and enough systemic interaction to generate emergent situations. If it is not fun at greybox, stop and rethink the design instead of compensating with content or visuals.
- **Sortable build, ~2 weeks.** A build that could realistically be prepared for release. The fundamental product exists: stable core gameplay, playable multiplayer, sufficient content/replayability, appropriate visuals, audio, basic UI, error handling, acceptable performance on target hardware, no critical blockers, working build/distribution pipeline.
- **Steam release, ~1 month after the sortable build.** That month covers Steam setup, store page, trailer, screenshots and capsules, marketing, creator/streamer outreach, playtesting, bug fixing, performance optimization, final polish, wishlist acquisition and release prep.

The timeline is an aggressive target, not a reason to ship a fundamentally broken game.

## 6. Core principle
Optimize for learning speed and market validation, not development volume. Every major development effort should answer one of: Is it fun? Is it different enough? Will players understand why they should play it? Will they want another round? Can they create memorable situations? Is it technically viable? Can we produce it within our constraints? Does it have commercial potential?

When an element does not meaningfully serve these questions, challenge it before implementing.
